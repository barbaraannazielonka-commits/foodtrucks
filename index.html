What is a food truck, really?

Before you take your first order, read this short introduction to the food truck business.

A food truck is a mobile kitchen: a vehicle or trailer fitted out with cooking equipment, storage, and a serving window, that sells food directly to customers on the street, at events, or outside offices and schools. The idea is old. Chuck wagons fed cowboys on cattle drives in the 1800s, and ice cream vans have circled neighbourhoods for generations. But the modern gourmet food truck, the kind serving restaurant-quality food from a small window, is often traced back to trucks like Kogi BBQ in Los Angeles around 2008. Since then, the trend has spread to cities across the world, including here in Norway.

Why the business model works

Starting a food truck usually costs far less than opening a restaurant. There is no long lease to sign, no large dining room to furnish, and no big staff to pay before a single dish is sold. A truck can also move to where the customers are: a business park at lunchtime, a festival on the weekend, a football match on a Saturday. For a young chef or entrepreneur, a truck is often the cheapest way to test a menu idea and build a following before committing to something bigger.

Why it is also hard work

A tiny kitchen means a short menu, careful planning, and almost no room for mistakes. Owners need permits to park and trade, must follow the same food safety rules as any restaurant, and depend heavily on weather, events, and word of mouth. Equipment failures, like a broken generator or a fridge that stops cooling, can end a day of trading in minutes. Many successful truck owners say the job is less about cooking and more about logistics: knowing exactly where to be, when, and with how much food prepared in advance.

Where your skills fit in

Everything you learn in restaurant and food processing studies applies here, just in a smaller space. A food truck still needs a planned menu, correct portioning, accurate costing, safe food handling, and good service at the window. If anything, the smaller scale makes those skills more visible: there is nowhere to hide a mistake when your whole kitchen is four square metres.

Low entry cost

A truck typically costs much less to set up than a full restaurant, which is why many first-time owners start here.

Mobile by design

Trucks follow the customers: lunch crowds on weekdays, festivals and markets on weekends.

Same rules, smaller space

Food safety, hygiene, and portion control still apply in full, even in a kitchen the size of a closet.
